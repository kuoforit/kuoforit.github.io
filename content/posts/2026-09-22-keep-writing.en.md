Whether a journal should be kept by hand or typed is an argument that never really ends.

Here's what I think: if it feels meaningful to you, if it feels right, just do it, and don't worry about what anyone else says. What actually matters was never the tool. It's whether you can build a habit of daily output through the process, and through steady accumulation and iteration, become a better version of yourself.

Handwriting has something no other method can replace. Studies say it reconnects parts of the brain tied to memory, and there's something about the friction of pen against paper that confirms you exist, that you're thinking, turning the chaos in your head into something concrete on the page. In an age when almost nobody picks up a pen anymore, being able to write Traditional Chinese characters well is, on its own, something worth holding onto.

But when it comes to speed and range, typing wins by a wide margin. The more practical question is which method you can actually sustain. No matter how good a tool is, if you give up on it, you're left with nothing.

Today I watched a video by Ali Abdaal, where he talked about something called Splurge: ten minutes a day, no backspace, no editing, sometimes typing with his eyes closed, letting the words come straight out of his stream of consciousness. Once it's done, he hands it to Claude to clean up. He described it as a kind of handcraft, a way of writing that still has texture to it. He's written his weekly newsletter for eight years without missing once, and calls this his most satisfying, most effortless method so far. Low friction, catches more of what's actually there, and through heavy volume of output, you slowly converge on a voice that's your own.

Some people would say using AI to organize a journal is cheating, that a real journal should stay raw and untouched. But I want to ask: why? We use calculators to help with math, dictionaries to fix our spelling. Why can't AI help us think and express ourselves? The point of a tool is to extend what a person can do, not to limit what's possible.

So my process goes roughly like this: use Splurge to capture the feeling of the moment, hand it to AI to polish, then read back the version of myself from that moment. Three steps, three different versions of "me" in conversation with each other. This isn't cheating. It's building a more three-dimensional way of knowing myself.

What is a journal actually for, in the end? Is it to leave behind a perfect piece of writing, or to watch yourself change through the act of writing? If it's the latter, the tool really doesn't matter. What matters is whether you're being honest with yourself.

Reading other people's writing is its own kind of practice. Beyond picking up knowledge, you can catch a sense of a writer's character, even their state of mind in that moment, from between the lines. It's not just reading what they said. It's closer to sitting with someone through a piece of their time. That's a strange, particular kind of experience.

After all this, I might wake up tomorrow doubting whether the whole method is too much trouble. That's fine too. Doubt, in its own way, is a form of honesty.
